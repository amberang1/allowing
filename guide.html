01 / Releasing feelings

The three Sedona questions

Write a feeling or the situation bringing it up. Notice what you actually feel: anger, sadness, tightness, heaviness or something harder to name.

Let yourself feel it, then ask one question at a time.

Could I let this feeling go?
Would I?
When?

Answer honestly and leave a little space between questions. Yes, no and unsure are all possible responses. Then choose another round or return home.

The pencil illustration is a way to picture holding and releasing a feeling. The cloud illustration can help you allow it to be present.

02 / Releasing wants

Larry Crane’s six-step method

This path guides the first three steps: choose inner freedom, decide to release, then identify and release the want. The remaining three steps describe how to keep practising. See all six steps.

Here, “wanting” means the feeling of lacking something or needing it before you can be okay. Look for whichever of these fits your reaction.

Approval

“I need them to like me.” You may be looking for praise, agreement, reassurance or proof that you’re enough.

Control

“This has to go my way.” You may be insisting that someone, a result or a feeling must change.

Safety or security

“I need to know it will be okay.” You may be looking for certainty or a guarantee before you can relax.

After choosing a want, the questions explore wanting it and the experience of having it. For example, with control:

Could I let go of wanting control, so I can have control?
Could I let go of wanting control to have control?
Which feels better — having control or wanting control?
Could I let go of wanting control, so I can have control?
Does it feel like I have control, or still want control?

Wait for your own answer. “Having” refers to the feeling you notice inside. If you still feel the lack, you can repeat. Practical decisions about your circumstances remain yours to make.

03 / Releasing by topics

Explore a topic

Most topics use attachment and aversion. Self-approval and exploring love have their own guided questions. Choose either at the top of the topic page, or read about them below.

Attachment and aversion

Choose a topic such as having money, being in a relationship or figuring things out. You can also write your own.

Attachment is clinging to something or feeling you must have it. Aversion is resisting something or wanting to avoid it.

Attachment

  1. Write what comes up about your attachment to the topic.
  2. Explore what feels advantageous about it.
  3. Explore what feels disadvantageous about it.
  4. Imagine never being able to experience it and notice your reaction.
  5. Explore letting go of the attachment.

Aversion

Explore your reactions, what you like and dislike about the topic, then what comes up when you imagine always experiencing it. Finally, explore letting go of the aversion.

At each question, work with the reaction that comes up. Choose approval, control or safety, then use the wanting-and-having sequence. Your topic, reaction and chosen want stay visible while you release.

04 / Releasing goals

Keep the goal. Work with the reaction.

Hale Dwoskin teaches using a goal statement to bring up feelings you can release. Read the goal, notice the first thought or feeling, release that feeling, then read the goal again.

I allow myself to [have, enjoy or experience one outcome].

Write in the present, describe what you choose to experience and include yourself. Keep it to one clear goal. Choose something that feels possible, or start with a smaller step. Leave room for different routes and other people’s choices.

  1. Read the goal slowly. For example, “I allow myself to enjoy a loving relationship.”
  2. Notice your first reaction. It might be excitement, doubt, “I don’t deserve it,” or a worry about what having it would mean.
  3. Feel the reaction. If it begins as a thought, notice the feeling or sensation that comes with it.
  4. Release that feeling. Use “Could I let this feeling go? Would I? When?” You can also explore wanting approval, control or security if that fits.
  5. Read the goal again. Work with the next reaction or stop when you choose.

Keep any practical action list separate from the goal statement. Releasing can be part of working toward a goal; outcomes also depend on circumstances and actions.

Examples you can use

  • Love — I allow myself to enjoy a loving, playful, committed partnership.
  • Savings — I allow myself to have and enjoy S$_____ in savings.
  • Business — I allow myself to enjoy S$_____ in monthly business profit.
  • Home — I allow myself to enjoy a peaceful home with room for a garden.
  • Creativity — I allow myself to enjoy sharing writing that matters to me.
Work with a goal
05 / Quick release

Welcome the feeling

This is the allowing approach. Bring your issue to mind, notice the feeling and let yourself feel it.

I allow this feeling to be here.

You can use those words as a reminder. If “I want it gone” comes up, allow that reaction too. Stay with the feeling as long as you choose, whether it changes or stays the same.

The cloud illustration can help: imagine the feeling as a cloud in an open sky. It can move, change shape or stay for a while.

06 / Triple welcoming

Welcome all three parts

Hale Dwoskin’s Triple Welcoming explores the experience, the urge to change it and the identity attached to it. Write a focus, then take one part at a time.

  1. Allow the experience. Notice the feelings, thoughts, memories and sensations that come up. Let them be present.
  2. Allow the urge to change it. Notice wanting to fix, resist or get rid of the experience. Allow that urge too.
  3. Allow the sense of “me”. Notice if the issue feels like part of who you are: “I’m a failure,” for example. Allow that sense of identity without needing to believe it.

If you don’t notice one of these parts, there’s nothing to invent. Welcoming your present experience leaves you free to take practical action.

Self-approval

Find this practice under Releasing by topics. Larry Crane uses releasing self-disapproval and giving yourself approval as a pair. This practice uses those questions instead of attachment and aversion.

Bring a moment of self-criticism to mind and notice what you feel. Then explore:

Could I let go of disapproving of myself right now?
Could I give myself approval, for no reason?

Wait for your own answer. You don’t have to argue that you’re wonderful, prove your worth or force yourself to feel positive. Repeat if you choose.

Explore self-approval

Exploring love

Find this practice under Releasing by topics. Lester Levenson explored his own experience of loving and the pain of needing love from someone else. Here, the guided questions explore that distinction and whether a loving feeling is available now.

  1. Bring yourself, another person or a situation to mind. Notice your actual reaction.
  2. Explore whether you’re wanting love or approval, then whether you can release that want.
  3. Try the question: “Could I change this feeling to love?”

The first two prompts help you approach the exploration; they aren’t a fixed sequence prescribed by Lester. If love isn’t available, allow the feeling that is here. You can return another time.

You can want connection and keep your boundaries. This practice doesn’t require approving of someone’s behaviour, forgiving them or contacting them.

What does releasing feel like?

You might notice a small easing, a less insistent thought or less need to make something change. Sometimes the feeling changes; sometimes you don’t notice a clear shift.

There’s no particular bodily sensation you have to produce. A completely empty mind, instant happiness or a dramatic release isn’t a requirement. You can leave the feeling alone without checking it constantly.

The questions and reminders direct your attention to your experience. Repeating the words until you’re convinced isn’t necessary. Answer honestly, allow a little space and notice what happens.

You can do another round or return to your day. A feeling coming back is something you can work with again; it doesn’t need to become a judgment about how well you’re doing.

The six steps

  1. Choose inner freedom. Value imperturbability — being at ease — more than holding on to approval, control or security.
  2. Decide to release. Decide that you can use the method and be imperturbable.
  3. Identify and release the want. Notice whether approval, control or security fits, then use the wanting-and-having questions.
  4. Keep returning to releasing. Use it through the day as reactions come up.
  5. If stuck, release wanting to control the stuckness. Notice the effort to make releasing happen.
  6. Continue releasing. Larry Crane describes increasing happiness and lightness. Notice your actual experience as you practise.

Courage, acceptance and peace

These words describe more open states in Larry Crane’s approach. See which, if any, fits what you notice.

Courageousness

“I am willing.” You may feel ready to listen, act or try another approach. “I allow myself to…” or “I can…” are goal openings associated with courageousness.

Acceptance

“I can let this moment be.” You may feel less need to argue with what has already happened. “I have…” is a goal opening associated with acceptance.

Peace

“For now, I can rest.” You may notice quiet or ease. “I am…” is a goal opening associated with peace.

Choose the goal wording you can work with. Enjoy pleasant feelings when they come. If you notice a need to keep them, you can release that need too.

When you feel stuck

  • If the answer is no, answer honestly and continue when you choose.
  • If nothing changes, notice any pressure to get a result. Try “Could I let go of wanting to control this stuckness?”
  • If you feel blank, start with the blankness itself. You don’t need to create a feeling.
  • If you keep analysing, try releasing the need to figure this out for a moment. You can return to practical questions later.
  • If you’re trying to get rid of the feeling, allow that urge too.
  • If it feels too much, stop and look around the room. Tap the logo to return home whenever you want.
